LitReX · a reading machine for the biomedical literature

The answers are already written down. No one has read them all.

Each year the medical literature grows by more than a million papers — a library no single lifetime can hold. Buried in that ocean of sentences are the ties between our genes and our diseases. This is an instrument built to read them.

the descent begins below

01 The library of a million hands

A cosmos of sentences

Consider the sum of what we have written about ourselves — every clinical study, every experiment, every careful observation of a cell under a lamp. It is one of the great achievements of our species: a memory kept outside of any one mind, so that those who come after need not learn it all again.

But it has grown beyond us. Read one paper every five minutes, day and night without pause, and the biomedical literature — tens of millions of papers — would take you more than three hundred years to reach the end. And it grows faster than anyone can read it. The knowledge is not lost, only unread — scattered across too many pages, in too many journals, for any single lifetime to gather.

✦ An aside · the tyrant in the name

Reading by ancient light

A tyrant lizard hides in the name: LitReX, with a T. rex curled inside it. The sky keeps the joke honest — the last Tyrannosaurus looked up about 66 million years ago, and light from a star that many light-years away is only now reaching us.

To read the whole literature is a journey of the same depth — measured not in light-years, but in all the years we have spent writing down what we know.

02 One kind of sentence

Where a gene meets a disease

A scientist searching for the roots of an illness is really hunting for one kind of sentence: the kind that names a gene and a disease in the same breath — and says something about how the two are bound together.

03 Five ways a gene can speak

The vocabulary of a relationship

Not every mention is a claim. We taught the machine to sort each sentence into one of five signals — a small, honest vocabulary for how a gene and a disease can be connected. The number beside each is how reliably it recognises that signal on pages it has never seen before.

One verdict turns out to be the commonest of all: in roughly half the sentences where a gene and a disease meet, no relationship is being claimed at all. Telling coincidence from meaning is much of the work.

More of the gene, more of the disease — it drives, promotes, or worsens.

04 Teaching a machine to read

From a careful few to the countless many

We could not hand-label ten million papers. So we did what teachers have always done: we chose a small, careful set of examples — 2,374 sentences drawn from Open Targets and the GWAS Catalog — and used them to teach a machine that already understood the language of biology.

Then came the test that keeps us honest. We showed it four of every five examples and hid the fifth. Every score on this page is measured on those hidden pages — the ones it was never allowed to study.

ii

Draw a representative handful — enough to teach from, small enough to label with care.

Fold thousands of phrasings down into the five clear signals.

v

Train

Teach a biomedical language model to tell the five signals apart.

vi

Read at scale

Point the finished instrument at the whole library.

A good instrument tells you where it is blind. This one barely recognises a negative finding — a study that searched for a link and found none — because we could gather only a handful of such examples to teach it. That is a limit of what we had to show it, not a flaw in its reasoning. And we say so plainly.

There was a subtler lesson, too. Left to its instincts, the machine grew fixated on the rare, dramatic answers and kept missing the commonest one — that most sentences, in the end, claim nothing. We taught it a kind of humility. Once it learned to trust the ordinary case, its reading of the whole literature grew markedly clearer — the everyday sentence went from barely a third caught to two-thirds caught.

06 The scale to come

An instrument pointed at everything

What we have built reads a few hundred sentences in a heartbeat. The same instrument, turned on the whole library, becomes something else entirely: a way to survey all of human medical writing at once, and to feed what it finds into the open maps of gene and disease that researchers use to reach for new medicines.

That full sky is not yet charted — reading every sentence ever written is the work still ahead. But the telescope is built, and it has been shown to see true.

We are a species that writes down what it learns, so the next generation can begin where this one left off. A machine that can finally read all of it is not a replacement for that impulse. It is its fulfilment.

✦ For the technically minded

The method, in full

The wonder is real; so is the machinery. For readers who want the vocabulary of the field, here is exactly what was built — data, model, and every number measured on held-out data.

Methods & results
taskSupervised 5-class classification of target–disease relations from sentence / abstract text.
corpusTarget–disease co-occurrences from Open Targets (Europe PMC text-mined evidence) and the GWAS Catalog. Sentence-level match first, abstract-level fallback; PMID-keyed, recency-filtered.
silver labels2,374 triples labelled by LLM judges (OpenAI gpt-4.1 + Anthropic Claude): free-text relation phrase + confidence.
label closureFree text → 5 classes by embedding each relation (text-embedding-3-small) and assigning to the nearest fixed class prototype by cosine similarity — chosen over clustering because it keeps direction-of-effect (up vs down) separable.
encoderPubMedBERT (BiomedNLP-BiomedBERT-base-uncased-abstract-fulltext), fine-tuned.
inputTyped entity markers: [TGT] gene [/TGT] [DIS] disease [/DIS] text. Marker tokens added to the vocabulary; embeddings resized.
splitStratifiedGroupKFold grouped by publication (no PMID leakage across splits) and stratified by class → 80 / 10 / 10 (1,805 / 226 / 226). Asserted leak-free.
lossClass-weighted × per-sample confidence-weighted cross-entropy. Class weights = √-inverse-frequency (α = 0.5).
model selection5-fold CV on the train split; weight-scheme sweep — sqrt 0.503 > uniform 0.486 > inverse 0.472 (mean macro-F1). sqrt chosen (+0.031 over inverse, >2× the ±0.014 fold std).
test metricsHeld-out 10%: accuracy 0.628 · weighted-F1 0.619 · macro-F1 0.531. Per-class F1 — no_relationship 0.70 · upregulation 0.67 · downregulation 0.61 · association 0.57 · negative_finding 0.12.
inferenceLoad-once, GPU-batched softmax scorer. Standalone parquet CLI + a pandas_udf wrapper (load-once-per-executor) for PySpark scale-out. Validated: reproduces the training-time eval predictions row-for-row.
stackPython 3.11 · PyTorch · HF Transformers · scikit-learn. Trained on an NVIDIA A100 80GB (CUDA 12.8, cu128 build).
known limitsnegative_finding is under-trained (152 examples) → F1 0.12; a data gap, not a modelling one. Full-corpus inference pending (the merged pool must be regenerated).

About this prototype. Every figure here is real, measured on data the model was never trained on. It is an early instrument, not a finished product — shown to make the work legible to anyone, not only to those who build such things.
